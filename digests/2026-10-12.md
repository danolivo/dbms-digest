# DBMS Weekly — 2026-10-12 (week of Oct 12–18)

_PostgreSQL 19 RC1 shipped on 15 October, and the bug reports kept coming. The most serious this week is not new in 19:
CLUSTER and REPACK silently drop a row that the same transaction inserted and then updated inside a rolled-back
savepoint, on every branch from 14 to master. Two more wrong-results bugs in released branches came in: a chain of LEFT
JOINs since 16, and a hashed SubPlan reused after a rescan. Last week's antijoin bug is fixed. The release now has an
upgrade problem: three 19 commits stopped consulting LC_CTYPE, so text-search and pg_trgm indexes built by 18 miss rows
after pg_upgrade in ICU and builtin-collation databases. A libpq option that let any dblink user pin a backend in an
unkillable loop was reverted after RC1. PgBouncer 1.26.1 fixes a SCRAM authentication bypass. On the blogs, Materialize
replaced SERIALIZABLE with a READ COMMITTED design it proved linearizable in Lean, after SIRead locks collapsed its
metadata store at about 4,300 ops/s. Repeated EXPLAIN ANALYZE timings turn out to depend on `synchronize_seqscans`. And
the CommitFest grew by a net +31, the largest gain of any fully counted week so far._

## PostgreSQL

- **[PostgreSQL 19 RC1 released](https://www.postgresql.org/message-id/179206948458.381513.13393797786723736747%40wrigleys.postgresql.org)** — out on 15 October as planned. The announcement confirms **RC2 on 12 November**, the same day as the minor releases, which "will include a large number of fixes", and **GA on 19 November**. The libpq `servicefile` revert (see the mailing lists below) landed after RC1 was stamped, so RC2 will already differ from it visibly. *(PostgreSQL Global Development Group · pgsql-announce)* Changes since Beta 4:
    - fixes to REPACK (CONCURRENTLY), pg_plan_advice, the new planner optimisations that depend on whether an expression can be NULL, automatic logical decoding at `wal_level=replica`, and parallel autovacuum
    - postgres_fdw statistics import now runs as the foreign table's owner, not as the user running ANALYZE
    - `COPY (query) TO … (FORMAT json)` now uses the query's column names
- **[Building a linearizable log on READ COMMITTED isolation](https://materialize.com/blog/building-a-linearizable-log-on-read-committed/)** — Materialize keeps its storage metadata in a small, hot Postgres table used as a compare-and-set log. In June a self-managed deployment hit a metastable failure under SERIALIZABLE. A short slowdown let SIRead locks pile up into the hundreds of thousands, which made every insert's conflict check slower, and latency went from milliseconds to 30 s and stayed there until load was removed. On a db.r6g.2xlarge RDS reproduction, throughput collapsed above about 4,300 CaS/s. The redesign runs every operation as a single READ COMMITTED statement and rests on one invariant: a shard's sequence numbers are always contiguous. Then "the predecessor exists and the PK rejects its successor" means "expected is the head". `FOR KEY SHARE` on the predecessor orders appends against truncating DELETEs, and a never-truncated marker row fences stale first writers, because `NOT EXISTS` reads the snapshot while the PK check sees the live index. They modelled Postgres READ COMMITTED in Lean 4 and proved the design linearizable, including the mixed-version rollout. The same benchmark then reached about 13,000 CaS/s without collapsing; they ran out of load first. *(Petros Angelatos · materialize.com)* `[vendor blog — substantive]`
- **[EXPLAIN ANALYZE is a sample size of one](https://exobench.ai/blog/explain-analyze-is-a-sample-size-of-one)** — `SELECT id FROM events WHERE tenant_id = 42 LIMIT 20` on a 1.5M-row, 31,915-page table ran once in 77.97 ms and nine times in about 0.07 ms, with every buffer a shared hit each time. The cause is `synchronize_seqscans`. For a table larger than a quarter of shared_buffers, a new seqscan starts where the previous one last reported its position (reported every 16 pages), so every later run started just before this tenant's rows at the end of the heap. With the setting off, every run took 80.8 ms (stddev 1.3%). The same statement also returned different rows with nothing writing. His rule for reading repeated runs: a stddev/mean near √N means one run was different; around 0.6 and rising means state is accumulating. *(Alexander Ioffe · exobench.ai)*
- **[Zero-downtime Postgres upgrades with logical replication](https://tapoueh.org/blog/2026/10/zero-downtime-postgres-upgrades-with-logical-replication/)** — part 3 of the series, reproducible with docker-compose on 14.24, 16.15, 17.11, 18.6 and 19beta4 under about 50 commits/s of live traffic. *(Dimitri Fontaine · tapoueh.org)* What it measures:
    - **cutover:** writes stall for 181 ms (freeze with `default_transaction_read_only`, wait for a marker row, `setval` from `pg_sequences`, switch); rolling back over a live reverse subscription took 179 ms
    - **`pg_upgrade --link`:** time follows object count, not data size — 1.69 s for 1,000 rows, 1.64 s for 5M rows/591 MB, 6.47 s for 5,000 near-empty tables
    - **slots across pg_upgrade:** since 17 they carry over, but a publisher's slot is kept only if it has consumed all WAL
    - **PG19 `WAIT FOR LSN` on a logical subscriber doesn't work:** the standby modes fail with "recovery is not in progress", `primary_flush` gives a false positive, and `latest_end_lsn` advances on receipt, not apply; use `pg_replication_origin_status.remote_lsn` plus a marker row
    - **PG19 sequence replication** moves values only on demand (still stale 20 s after a change); splitting sequences by residue, even on one side and odd on the other, removes the `setval` race
    - pglogical's apply worker crashed in his run when partitioned parents were targets
- **[What gets TOASTed in Postgres](https://planetscale.com/blog/what-gets-toasted-in-postgres)** — a reproducible psql walk-through, prompted by the LZ4 TOAST default. TOAST starts once a row passes 2,032 bytes and works on the largest values first. Window size matters: on a 210 KB string drawn from 5,000 words, pglz (4 KB window) fails its 25%-savings test and stores the value raw, while LZ4 (64 KB window) saves 15%. On repetitive JSON pglz compresses better (19 MB vs 24 MB, from 103 MB) but LZ4 loads 1.5–3× faster. The non-obvious part is partial reads: `left(body,200)` over 100 × 1 MB values needs 301 page lookups and about 1 ms with pglz, but 6,234 lookups and about 16 ms with LZ4, which detoasts the whole value. A 200-byte read at offset 500,000 of a bytea: 0.6 ms with EXTERNAL, 43 ms with LZ4, 116 ms with pglz. *(Ahmed Darwich · planetscale.com)* `[vendor blog — substantive]`
- **[stack depth limit exceeded: four causes](https://now-next.nl/en/insights/postgresql-stack-depth-limit-exceeded/)** and **[Rails, ActiveRecord and RLS, measured](https://now-next.nl/en/insights/rails-activerecord-postgresql-row-level-security-tenant/)** — both on PG 17.11. *(Chris van Eijk · now-next.nl)*
    - a policy that reads its own table inline is caught at plan time as "infinite recursion detected"; the same subquery inside a function isn't, and recurses until the stack runs out (1,816 levels for SQL, 788 for PL/pgSQL). A SECURITY DEFINER helper owned by the table owner doesn't break the loop under FORCE; only a definer the policy doesn't apply to does
    - raising `max_stack_depth` from 2 to 7 MB lets runaway recursion go about 3.5× deeper, so it only fixes deep-but-finite cases: `1+1+…` fails at 4,093 terms, as do 4,093 chained views; nested ORs hit "memory exhausted" at 3,331 terms, while 300,000 flat ORs and a 1M-value IN list are fine
    - ActiveRecord sends nothing when a connection returns to the pool: a request that set no tenant saw the previous tenant's rows 200 times out of 200, and with `with_connection` 155–160 of 600 requests got the wrong tenant
- **[Old tech is sometimes better: a case study](https://www.depesz.com/2026/10/16/old-tech-is-sometimes-better-a-case-study-based-on-blogpost-from-now-next/)** — tests a now-next claim that logging only changed columns makes audit triggers smaller but slower. 100k-row bulk UPDATE, best of 3: no trigger 238 ms; jsonb full row 921 ms / 42 MB; jsonb diff via `jsonb_each` 1,430 ms / 15.8 MB; hstore full row 830 ms / 39.8 MB; hstore diff (`hstore(OLD) - hstore(NEW)`) **798 ms / 14.7 MB**. With hstore subtraction the diff is both the smallest and the fastest. *(Hubert 'depesz' Lubaczewski · depesz.com)*
- **[Finding (and fixing) a silent throughput ceiling in PostgreSQL](https://enterprisedb.com/blog/finding-and-fixing-silent-throughput-ceiling-postgresql)** — HammerDB TPROC-C on PG18, 50 warehouses: going from 64 to 128 virtual users dropped NOPM from about 400k to about 52k with CPU at 35%. More than 75 backends queued on `ExclusiveLock on extension of relation`, the longest for 6.7 s; more vCPU and faster NVMe changed nothing. Hash-partitioning the hot table 10 ways cut `Lock:extend` waits by 98% and gave 7.2× the throughput. *(Vishal Sawale · enterprisedb.com)* `[vendor blog]` `[unverified: hardware not disclosed; doesn't discuss PG16's multi-block relation extension]`
- **[Making COUNTs and the rest of search really fast (Part II)](https://www.paradedb.com/blog/opening-a-closed-tin-part-2)** — ParadeDB's next reply to PlanetScale's TIN (10-05 issue), this time with mechanisms. Visibility checks were 81% of the page accesses in a COUNT; partitioning LSM segments by ctid gives each one a heap-page range, and a segment whose whole range is all-visible skips per-row checks (1,382 → 30 page accesses). A heap-page → first-DocId map replaces a stored ctid per document (after 1,000 updates, about 7,500 → under 400 buffer accesses), and terms in more than 10% of documents switch to bitmap postings, halving p99. Claims 2.0× TIN on Wikipedia COUNT and 10–22× on filtered and time-sorted queries TIN's benchmarks don't cover, while conceding no vendor benchmark settles "fastest". *(Ming Ying · paradedb.com)* `[vendor blog — substantive]` `[unverified: vendor-run benchmark]`
- **[pg_vectorsearch](https://github.com/timescale/pg_vectorsearch)** — Tiger Data's new vector-index extension, alpha, PG18+; Postgres Weekly reports pgvectorscale moving to maintenance mode. *(Tiger Data · github.com/timescale)* `[unverified: vendor performance claims]`
    - PRISM, an IVF-style index with a hierarchical centroid tree kept in shared buffers; posting lists are RaBitQ-quantized with error bounds, so candidates are ruled out before reranking against full vectors in the heap; async I/O, AVX2/AVX-512/NEON
    - indexes existing pgvector `vector`/`halfvec` columns through binary-compatible types; lists never split on their own (`prism_rebalance` or REINDEX)
    - README warning: vectors over about 2 kB get TOASTed and the rerank pays a fetch the planner doesn't cost — use `STORAGE PLAIN`
    - on [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50086165) (210 pts, 53 comments) the authors said they left pgvectorscale's DiskANN-style graph because on low-dimensional data parts of it became unreachable and recall dropped silently; users found a 1,968-dimension cap and post-index filtering only

## Wider DBMS & distributed data

- **[Chasing that 32 ms tail latency in ClickHouse](https://clickhouse.com/blog/chasing-32ms-tail-latency-clickhouse)** — after 2 s idle, Linux resets cwnd to initcwnd (8 MSS here). With TCP_NODELAY, the Native protocol's separate ProfileEvents/Log/Progress writes overflowed that window, so the end-of-stream packet waited an extra RTT. Batching the non-urgent metadata packets into one write halved server PSH segments (50 → 25) and took the tail from 32 ms to about 1 ms, server-side only, since customers often can't change `tcp_slow_start_after_idle`. Reproduced with netns + netem. Relevant to any wire protocol that flushes many small messages per query. *(Kavi Kanagaraj · clickhouse.com)* `[vendor blog — substantive]`
- **[SurrealKV 1.0 replaces RocksDB in SurrealDB](https://surrealdb.com/blog/we-are-replacing-rocksdb-with-surrealkv-1-0)** — a Rust LSM engine with snapshot isolation. *(Tobie Morgan Hitchcock · surrealdb.com)* `[vendor blog — substantive]` `[unverified: single-run vendor benchmark, non-default SurrealKV settings]`
    - adaptive-radix-tree memtable in a fixed arena, where RocksDB and Pebble use skiplists
    - a "commit ring": committers validate in parallel, then one flusher orders a group and appends the WAL with at most one fsync
    - differential testing against an in-memory model, 2.5M ops per CI run
    - with sync durability vs RocksDB: point reads 7.95×, updates 6.10×; without sync, RocksDB writes 1.9–2.4× faster, and peak memory is 1.79 vs 1.02 GiB
- **[YSQL Connection Manager is GA](https://www.yugabyte.com/blog/ysql-connection-manager-is-now-ga/)** — a transaction-mode pooler built into each node of a Postgres-derived engine. Instead of the pooler parsing SQL, the query layer reports every session-state change — SETs inside functions, SETs undone by rollback, ALTER ROLE/DATABASE … SET — and that state is replayed on whichever backend runs the next transaction. LISTEN, temp tables, WITH HOLD cursors, SQL PREPARE and SET ROLE still pin the session. Compare last week's CF #7415 (extensible ReadyForQuery for poolers). *(Suranjan Kumar · yugabyte.com)* `[vendor blog — substantive]` `[unverified: vendor numbers]`
- **[Testing DuckDB 2.0 on our Iceberg datasets](https://lakehousebox.com/blog/duckdb-2-on-iceberg-measured/)** — TPC-H SF10 on Iceberg, 2.0 preview vs 1.5.6. *(Javier de la Torre · lakehousebox.com)*
    - same data centre, cold: 105.9 → 62.7 s (1.93× per-query geomean), the same ~12 GB read over 52% more GETs
    - over home fibre: 1.04×, with some queries pulling 2–3× the bytes from over-eager prefetch
    - warm runs regress: 1.5.6 re-reads nothing, 2.0 re-fetches 170 MB, and q09/q21 are slower even with zero network
    - Iceberg writes go from 409 files of ~5 MB to 4 of ~536 MB, which explains part of the read gain
- **[Why sorted Parquet files don't guarantee an ordered scan in DataFusion](https://cdelmonte.dev/explainers/sorted-files-ordered-scan/)** — traced through DataFusion 55.1.0: a scan partition concatenates its files instead of merging them, so declared order (`WITH ORDER`, or `sorting_columns` read only from the first row group) survives only if each file's max ≤ the next file's min. Overlapping ranges silently drop the ordering and the GROUP BY falls back to hashing; neither declaration nor metadata is verified, so a wrong one gives wrong results. The same question as Append vs MergeAppend over sorted partitions. *(Christian Del Monte · cdelmonte.dev)*
- **[Redis now has compressed bitmaps (Roaring)](https://lemire.me/blog/2026/10/15/redis-now-has-compressed-bitmaps-roaring/)** — an opt-in `bitmap-roaring` encoding due in Redis 8.12. The SETBIT string encoding allocates up to the highest offset, so one bit at offset 4 billion costs 512 MB and every BITOP scans all of it. *(Daniel Lemire · lemire.me)*
- **[How we cut a ClickHouse delete from 12 s to under 2](https://plutus-cloud.com/blog/clickhouse-partitioning-for-delete-and-reinsert-syncs)** — a lightweight DELETE walks every partition whatever its predicate matches, so one touching a single monthly partition was no faster than a full-window one (15–17 s). Yearly partitions brought p50 to 0.7–1.8 s; one-month reads then scanned about 7× the rows until the month moved into the sort key. *(plutus-cloud.com)*

## Commercial engines (SQL Server, Oracle, MySQL, …)

- **[When a small change writes too much: large folios vs mmap in PXC/Galera](https://www.percona.com/blog/when-a-small-change-writes-too-much-how-a-linux-kernel-improvement-can-strike-you-in-pxc-galera-workloads/)** — after PXC 5.7→8.0 plus Debian 11→12 (kernel 5.10 → 6.1), Galera threads produced write bursts about every 33 s — 13 GB from appliers and 5.2 GB from the receiver in a few seconds — with InnoDB and binlog flat. *(Przemysław Malkowski · percona.com)*
    - gcache is mmap'd, and on XFS with large folios (5.18+) a dirty mmap page writes back the whole folio, up to 2 MB, instead of 4 KB; 6.6's per-block dirty tracking helps write()/pwrite() but not mmap
    - large readahead on RAID makes the folios bigger
    - workarounds: ext4 (folios only from 6.16), lower `read_ahead_kb`, `MADV_RANDOM`, or pwrite instead of mmap
- **[MariaDB: 3.5× InnoDB regression on IN-list point lookups fixed (MDEV-37992)](https://jira.mariadb.org/browse/MDEV-37992)** — a 100-value PK IN-list ran at about a third of 10.4's QPS from 10.5 on, with 18× more context switches per query. The fix stops nearly every access from moving the page to the head of the buffer-pool LRU (121M pages made "young" vs 343K after). The Foundation's [reproduction](https://mariadb.org/mariadb-foundation-reproducing-and-validating-mdev-37992-with-taf/) measured 18–19k → 66–68k TPS on 11.8, 12.3 and 13.1. *(Mark Callaghan; Alessandro Vetere · MariaDB JIRA / mariadb.org)*
- **[Fact-checking Microsoft's "PostgreSQL to SQL Field Notes: Connections"](https://www.brentozar.com/archive/2026/10/fact-checking-microsofts-postgresql-to-sql-field-notes-connections/)** — Microsoft's series for Postgres developers moving to its "SQL" products claims 32,767 connections per instance. True, he says, only for connections that do nothing: what limits work is worker threads — 512 up to 4 cores, 512 + (cores − 4) × 16 up to 64 — and a parallel query takes DOP threads plus a coordinator per concurrent branch. His demo query on a 4-core box took 250 of the 512. Blocked queries keep their threads, so a THREADPOOL stall locks out monitoring too. A useful counterweight in the process-per-connection debate. *(Brent Ozar · brentozar.com)*
- **[Thread pool in Percona Server and MySQL, part 2](https://www.percona.com/blog/thread-pool-in-percona-server-and-mysql-part-2/)** — follows part 1, now on Community MySQL 26.7.0. At 2,560 connections with a 2 GB buffer pool, Percona's pool does 2,304 TPS against Oracle MySQL's 371; fully cached, the order flips (19,054 vs 15,519 TPS). Percona's is very sensitive to `thread_pool_size`/`oversubscribe`; Oracle's helps only from 1,280 connections. *(Bogdan Degtyariov · percona.com)* `[single runs]`
- **[How we solved a rare Hyperdrive MySQL caching bug](https://blog.cloudflare.com/hyperdrive-mysql/)** — protocol traps for a pooler: `utf8` is utf8mb3; `COM_RESET_CONNECTION` resets the charset to the *server default*, not the one negotiated at handshake; mysql2 lacks `CLIENT_DEPRECATE_EOF`, so the proxy rewrites result-set terminators per client. The 0.001% empty-result bug came from two caches sharing a store in different capability encodings — a Postgres-era assumption that cached bytes equal bytes sent. *(Pranoy Dutta · blog.cloudflare.com)* `[vendor blog — substantive]`

## Research & cutting edge

- **[The case for single-version concurrency control in the cloud](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15793)** — a CIDR'27 position paper: with analytics offloaded to replicas or columnar engines, the OLTP writer no longer needs MVCC, whose version chains, GC and 16–32 B headers are all cost; rare long transactions get on-demand snapshot indexes. The only evidence is code size from LeanStore prototypes (303 vs 426 lines of core code); the ~35K-line figure for Postgres MVCC is "AI-estimated", and there are no performance numbers. *(Nguyen, Haas, El-Hindi, Leis, Bernstein)* _[paper]_
- **[JetStream: generating verified query accelerators for existing DBMSs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20527)** — LLM agents generate per-template accelerators over the host's own tables; on PG 18.4 changes are captured with statement-level triggers and transition tables, and verified variants carry Verus proofs. The headline 315× over DuckDB on TPC-H SF20 comes almost entirely from incrementally maintained per-query state — generated IVM, at 28.4 GB resident; stateless accelerators manage 2.1× over DuckDB and 4.4× over PG. *(Balasingam, Ngom, Yu, Kraska)* _[paper]_
- **[Join order enumeration for double-star and helix join graphs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15187)** — two star schemas sharing a spoke get a closed-form count of join orders and an exact C_out optimiser in O(a log a + b log b + ab) that beats DPhyp; the helix gets an exact count, but there the generic DP wins. *(Taneja, Marcus)* _[paper]_
- **[ARCADE: retrofitting ABAC onto databases with an external policy engine](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18660)** — PostgreSQL and MySQL plugins send authorisation decisions to Cedar while the engine enforces them, on top of native DAC/RBAC; TPC-C overhead 1.3% on PG, 3.9% on MySQL. *(Yelisetty, Sural et al.)* _[paper]_
- **[Recall-bounded staleness for filtered multi-tenant ANN graph indexes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15130)** — splits recall loss into staleness and filtered navigability; on a merge-based graph, staleness "anti-amortizes" (20× larger merges cut recall@10 from 99.44% to 99.01%), and per-tenant staleness ceilings on FreshDiskANN remove tight-tier SLA violations. Relevant to background maintenance of vector indexes. *(Das, Devadkar, Godbole)* _[paper]_
- **[Benchmarking content-defined chunking for deduplication](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13704)** — an ICDE 2027 paper that finds a long-standing parameterisation error in AE, copied across papers and tools, which makes chunks 13–39% smaller than intended and inflates its reported dedup ratio. *(Gregoriadis, Balduf, Scheuermann, Pouwelse)* _[paper]_
